How to read it

1. Check what kind of hand you have. If you have an Ace that can count as 11 without busting, it's a soft hand (A,6 is soft 17, for example). If you have two equal cards, it's a pair. Everything else is a hard hand.

2. Find your row and the column of the dealer's up card. Face cards (J, Q, K) count as 10.

3. Play what the cell says. Always, even if your gut says otherwise: the chart already did the math on millions of hands for you.

The 6 plays that save the most money

  • Always split aces and eights. Two aces together are a weak 12; 8,8 is 16, the worst hand in the game.
  • Never split tens or fives. 20 almost always wins, and 5,5 is a 10: better to double it.
  • Double 11 against almost everything. There are so many 10-value cards that it's your best chance to put more money in.
  • Stand on 12 against 4, 5 or 6. The dealer is weak: let them take the risk.
  • Hit 16 against 10. It hurts, but standing loses even more.
  • Don't take insurance. Without counting cards, it's a losing bet in the long run.

Why it works

The house edge comes from a single rule: if you bust, you lose even if the dealer busts afterwards. Basic strategy doesn't remove it, but shrinks it to the minimum: it tells you when to take risks (double, split) and when to let the dealer take them.

It's pure math: each cell compares how much you win or lose on average with every possible play and keeps the best one. That's why, even if a single hand goes wrong, always playing it this way pays off the most in the long run.

Frequently asked questions

Does basic strategy guarantee winning?

No. It cuts the house edge to the minimum (about 0.5%), but the house still has the edge. It's the foundation for playing well, and the foundation for counting cards.

Does it work at any casino?

It works for 4- to 8-deck games. The only thing that changes a few cells is whether the dealer hits or stands on soft 17: pick your table's option above the chart.

Do I have to memorize it?

Yes, to use it at a real table (you can't use your phone at the table). It's quick to learn in parts: pairs first, then soft hands and hard hands last. In Edge 21 the chart lights up as you learn it.
